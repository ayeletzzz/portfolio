Lead Handling Agent

The first AI agent to sell cars on a dealership’s behalf, no human in the loop. In its first 30 days it handled 5,435 leads and set 243 appointments.

2025-2026 Product Designer 0→1 Agent Design Web SaaS

The Challenge

Dealerships were spending millions to acquire leads and losing half of them before anyone picked up the phone. Leads go cold in minutes, not hours.

The moment a shopper submits a form, the probability of reaching them starts to collapse. Most BDC teams can't respond within the OEM's 10-minute window.

Fullpath's CDP already held rich profiles on every shopper: vehicle interest, past interactions, engagement signals.

The opportunity was to connect that data to an agent that could act on it the moment a lead came in, 24/7, and hand off to a salesperson only when it actually mattered.

Starting point

I started this in June 2025. Agent design was not a thing yet. There was nothing to reference.

So before designing anything, I visited beta dealerships in person — I wanted to see how people actually used the product. Three things changed everything.

Finding 01

The inbox was the interface

Managers weren't checking the dashboard. They lived in email. The product was solving for a workflow that wasn't theirs.

→ How do you make the hand-off seamless for two completely different people?

Finding 02

Transparency, not replacement

Reps weren't afraid of the agent taking their job. They were afraid of not knowing what it had already said to the customer.

→ How do you build trust in an agent talking to strangers who do not know it is AI?

Finding 03

Control without language

Dealers wanted to customize agent behavior but couldn't describe how. They needed guided defaults — not a blank prompt box.

→ How do you let dealers shape the agent's behavior without letting them break it?

The Hypothesis

If the agent felt like a team member rather than a tool, dealers would extend it the same trust they’d give a new hire. Not blind trust. Earned trust. Someone you brief, watch for the first few weeks, and gradually hand more responsibility to as they prove themselves.

The Solution

Three decisions that shaped the product.

Decision 01

The agent needed a name, a face, and a job title.

We did not introduce it as AI. It joined the dealership as a named team member. Dealers chose the name, the avatar, the tone. Every message came from that identity — shoppers responded to a person, not a system.

Decision 02

Visibility was the product, not a feature.

The biggest anxiety managers had wasn't about the agent's performance — it was not knowing what it was saying on their behalf. I designed three layers so sales teams always knew exactly where every lead stood.

Decision 03

The rep always had a seat at the table.

Every conversation the agent handled was visible in real time, not as a log, not after the fact. Reps could read the thread as it unfolded and step in mid-conversation if something needed a human touch. One tap to take over. No explanation required.

Decision 04

Dealers needed to talk to the agent, not configure it.

We tried giving dealers a text field to edit agent instructions directly — they'd overwrite compliance guardrails without realizing it. So Omni replaced configuration entirely: describe what you want in plain language, and the agent translates it.

Craft

Real conversations handled autonomously 24/7, in the dealer's voice.

Field Research

We shipped V1 to five beta dealerships, then flew out to watch people actually use it.

Three things came up at every single site.

Reps had no idea what the agent had said or when. They’d pick up a lead mid-thread with zero context, sometimes doubling up on outreach the agent had already done, sometimes saying something that contradicted it entirely.

They also needed a way to claim individual leads. Not a global off switch, a per-conversation one. Some leads were relationships. Some were complaints. They needed to be able to say “this one’s mine” without disrupting everything else.

They liked it. They just weren’t sure they trusted it yet, not because it had failed them, but because they hadn’t seen it handle enough on its own to stop hovering.

Iteration

How Design Your Agent got to where it is.

V1   No visibility

Controls with no window into agent behavior.

These are the raw AI prompts running the agent. Everything it says, how it follows up, how it handles inventory, all in here. Fully exposed, fully editable, with no guidance on what changing them would actually do.

Result: Dealers felt accountable for outcomes they couldn't actually shape.

V2   Layer leak

Visible behavior — but the system layer wasn't protected.

This is the system layer, the agent’s default behavior, set by Fullpath. Dealers can read it, but shouldn’t be able to change it.

This is the dealer’s layer, where they add their own rules on top.

Result: Dealers edited default prompts directly. Agent contradicted itself.

In practice, dealers weren’t breaking the product on purpose. They just wanted the agent to behave their way, and this was the only lever they had.

V3   Shipped ✓

Plain language in. Guardrails stay invisible.

Dealers describe what they want in plain language. Omni asks clarifying questions, confirms changes, and handles the translation.

This is what the agent actually runs on. Generated by Omni, read-only for dealers. They can see it, they just can’t touch it.

Result: Dealers describe how they sell. Omni translates. System layer is untouchable.

Obstacle

94% of page views happened in Conversations. Not Performance. Not Design Your Agent.

Users were living in Conversations. The trust problem wasn't a dashboard problem — it was a conversations problem. I mapped five user flows and found three that were critically broken, all with the same root cause: the gap between something is happening and I understand it.

Redesign 01

The conversation list became a command center.

1,500+ conversations, reorganized around what needs a human.

Three tabs restructured the queue.

All, Needs You, AI Active. Urgent vs. routine, visible at a glance — without searching.

Every conversation has an Omni summary.

One AI-generated line answers why this needs your attention, so reps don't have to read the whole thread.

Every control surfaced.

Numbers, channels, per-lead AI mode — Full, Reactive, Off. Explicit, not buried. Trust in AI requires transparency.

Redesign 02

Performance became a conversation too.

Four numbers. Everything else is a question.

Stripped to four numbers.

Stopping the Clock, Avg Response Time, Appt Set Rate, After-Hours Leads — each compared to the previous period.

Everything else moved into Omni.

GMs ask in plain language; Omni pulls the data. Suggested questions replaced the sections we removed — and solved onboarding almost by accident.

One interface, two mental models.

Reps track individual conversations. GMs ask questions about the whole team. Omni handles both without switching screens.

What I'd do differently

01

Giving dealers partial control was sometimes worse than giving them none.

V2 felt like the right call. Dealers could see the system behavior and add their own layer on top. But some started editing the defaults, which broke things in ways they could not predict. They felt responsible for the agent's behavior without actually having the right tools to shape it. I should have tested the line between visibility and editability much earlier.

02

The email was the real product for most managers, and I did not treat it that way.

I built the dashboard as the primary experience and the email as a notification. Visiting dealerships made it obvious that managers were living in their inbox. If I started over, I would design the email digest first and build the platform as the deeper layer.

03

I would measure trust, not just conversion.

We tracked appointments and revenue. We did not track how often reps let the agent continue versus stepping in themselves. That number would have told us much more about whether the handoff design was actually working.

04

We never heard from the shoppers.

We measured everything from the dealer's side. The moment that stuck with me most from beta was when an agent booked an appointment with no human involvement and the customer felt personally taken care of. We validated the outcome but never went deeper. Understanding how shoppers experienced the agent would have made every trust decision sharper.
